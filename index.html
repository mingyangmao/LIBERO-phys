Execution settings

One setting.
Opposite effects.

A policy predicts a sequence of actions, executes the first 5 or 10 control steps, then reads a new observation and predicts again. At 20 Hz that is 0.25 s or 0.50 s of simulated time between two replans.

For SmolVLA, replanning every 5 steps instead of 10 raised success on the slippery plate from 60% to 94%. On the drawer at 40 N of dry friction, success fell from 60% to 4%. Both comparisons use 50 matched starts and count success by step 300.

Benchmark design

Change one thing.
Pair everything else.

A drop in success can be attributed to a physical property only if nothing else moved. LIBERO-Phys keeps the scene, the initial state, the checkpoint and its noise seed fixed.

  1. Perturb at runtime

    Parameters are written into the compiled MuJoCo model after every reset. No LIBERO asset or source file changes; with the default values written back, the simulation steps exactly as unmodified LIBERO does. Mass and inertia are scaled together, so an object keeps its shape and mass distribution.

  2. Pair every trial

    Each condition uses up to 50 of LIBERO’s predefined initial states with the same noise seed per state. A Wilson 95% interval says how uncertain one success rate is. McNemar’s test, on the trials two conditions share, says whether two rates differ.

  3. Measure what the arm can do

    For the drawer, the arm’s near-static pull at full action was measured at about 55 N. It serves as a reference, not as a filter: the grid goes to 60 N, and every success rate counts all trials that were run. Feasibility is tested separately and reported next to the rates.

  4. Report the execution setting

    Replanning interval and step limit are part of every result. Most runs go to 600 steps and give the 300-step result from the same trials. Some earlier runs stopped at step 300; the charts say so where no 600-step result exists.

The two benchmarks answer different questions. RoboTwin-Phys measures how far success falls across a large task set when many physical attributes shift at once. LIBERO-Phys measures how success changes along one parameter on a few tasks. LIBERO-Plus perturbs object layout, camera viewpoint, robot initial state, language instruction, lighting, background and sensor noise. LIBERO-Dynamic moves the target object at controlled speeds, adds inference latency, and compares execution protocols and action horizons. Neither changes mass, friction or joint resistance.

Questions? Answers.

Why change one parameter at a time?

Because the answer depends on the parameter and the task. SmolVLA keeps 90% success at 100× damping on a knob, falls to 54% at 30× damping on a drawer, and fails every trial at 1.6× friction on a plate. One score over jointly sampled parameters cannot show which property a policy fails on.

Parameters that the mechanics couple, such as mass and grasp friction, are also run together on a small grid.

Is a perturbed task still solvable?

That is tested, not assumed, and nothing is filtered. Every success rate on this page counts all trials that were run, including levels that may be out of the arm’s reach.

For the drawer, the arm’s near-static pull at full action was measured at about 55 N for this drawer and arm pose, and it falls with speed.3 We use it as a reference; the grid goes up to 60 N (109%). At 50 N, a scripted full-scale pull that takes over from a policy’s own grasp opened the drawer from 7 of the 10 initial states that the policy runs also cover. SmolVLA and π0.5 opened it from none of those 10. For those states the failure is the policy’s. At 60 N the scripted pull opened 2 of 10 within 300 steps, so most failures there are not.

For the microwave door, a scripted full-scale push closed the door from 11 of 20 initial states at 12 N·m of dry friction, where SmolVLA finished the task in 0/50 trials and π0.5 in 2/50.9

Why can two charts show different rates for the same condition?

Because some conditions were run more than once, and the two charts need different runs. The single-parameter chart takes, for each point, the run with the most trials, and of those the longer one. The replanning chart needs two runs with the same step limit, one per interval.

Example: π0.5 on the drawer at 40 N, replanning every 10 steps, success within 300 steps. The 600-step run gave 14/50 (single-parameter chart); the 300-step run gave 12/50 (replanning chart). π0.5 is not reproducible trial for trial across processes, so two runs of one condition differ by a few trials. Both charts say which run each number comes from: the readout under the single-parameter chart, and the table under the replanning chart.

Why does the mass grid go up to 32×?

LIBERO’s objects are far lighter than real ones. The bowl weighs 5.6 g and the plate 11.5 g. Ceramic pieces of the same size would weigh roughly 200 to 300 g; that figure is our estimate from the asset dimensions, not a measurement. At 32× the bowl weighs 180 g. In our runs, slipping and stalling only begin well above the default mass, so a grid that stopped at 2× would test nothing.

Are the comparisons paired?

Yes. Every condition draws on the same list of initial states, and each state has its own noise seed, so state 17 under 30× damping differs from state 17 under default physics only in the damping.

Not every level has all 50 states: fine sweeps used the first 20 and coarse sweeps the first 10. The charts show the count for every point. Where the text compares two levels with different counts, it uses the states both levels have and says so.

How reproducible is a trial?

Three different things are worth keeping apart.

Same start and seed. A trial is defined by an initial-state ID and a noise seed. All conditions share them. This always holds.

Step-for-step reproduction. Running a trial again gives exactly the same trajectory. Of the 167 videos on this page, 118 are identical, step for step, to the trial in the benchmark’s results.

Outcome match. The other 49 videos are re-recordings that end the way the original trial did (same success or failure, and for a success a step count within 10%) but differ along the way. Almost all come from the bottle and microwave scenes. There, the simulator’s wrist-camera image of one and the same state can differ between two renders by one intensity level in a few pixels; that changes the policy’s next actions, and after first contact the trial goes its own way. These videos say so under the player.

In the first round of re-runs (157 trials, 23 November 2026), 117 were identical step for step and 38 of the other 40 ended with the original outcome on the first attempt; the other 2 were repeated until it matched. In the second round (12 door trials, 24 November 2026), 2 were identical, 9 matched in outcome, and 1 did not match in 8 attempts and has no video. The success rates in the charts always come from the original runs, never from re-recordings.

π0.5 is not reproducible trial for trial across processes: two independent runs of the microwave task under default physics agreed on success or failure in 48 of 50 initial states.

Which policies and checkpoints?

Four published checkpoints from the Hugging Face hub, all run in the same simulator version and checked under default physics before any perturbation:

  • SmolVLA lerobot/smolvla_libero
  • π0.5 lerobot/pi05_libero_finetuned_v044
  • Fast-WAM, a world-action model, run in bfloat16 lerobot/fastwam_libero_uncond_2cam224
  • SmolVLA-Plus, SmolVLA trained on LIBERO-Plus data lerobot/smolvla_libero_plus
Does the benchmark modify LIBERO?

No asset or source file is edited. Parameters are written into the live MuJoCo model after each reset.

One fix is applied at reset time, also without touching LIBERO’s source. In tasks whose initial condition opens or switches on a fixture, such as the microwave task, LIBERO draws a growing number of random values on every reset, which moves the fixture by up to a centimeter from one reset to the next. The benchmark clears that list so that every reset places the fixtures identically.

What is not in the benchmark yet?

The table geometry group (tilt and height); a multi-factor split over all 40 LIBERO tasks, which will give one aggregate number comparable with other benchmarks; full grids for Fast-WAM and SmolVLA-Plus; and a fixed reference torque for the knob and the door, which is why their dry friction is shown in N·m. Robot-side parameters, cameras and object scale are out of scope.

  1. Success within 300 steps is the share of trials that reach the task’s goal in at most 300 control steps (15 s at 20 Hz); within 600 steps, 30 s. A trial ends at its first success. Runs made to 600 steps give both numbers from the same trials. Runs that stopped at step 300 have no 600-step result, unless every trial had already succeeded. Intervals are Wilson 95%.
  2. Plate friction is set to μ = 0.1, 0.3, 0.6, 0.95 (default) and 1.5, shown as rounded multiples of the default. MuJoCo takes the larger friction coefficient of two touching bodies, so the table’s coefficient is lowered until the plate’s own value governs the plate–table contact; this leaves the default rollouts unchanged. Dry friction in the drawer slide and the door hinge uses a nearly rigid friction constraint, so that the joint does not creep below the threshold. Drawer levels are also given as a share of the 55 N pull reference. Knob and door dry friction are in N·m; their reference torque is not fixed yet.
  3. At full action the arm’s operational-space controller pulls the drawer with F(v) ≈ 55 N − 213 v (v in m/s), measured for this drawer and one arm pose, with no joint torque saturating; 55 N is the near-static value. Contact logs give the gripper’s force along the slide axis: SmolVLA and π0.5 sustain about 42 N while the drawer moves, at every friction level logged.
  4. At 32× the bowl weighs mg ≈ 1.8 N. With the bowl’s surface coefficient at μ = 0.285, two fingers hold at most 2μF ≈ 1.4 N at the measured pinch force of about 2.5 N. The 0.3× and 0.6× columns give the bowl’s surface coefficient priority in every contact, the finger pads included; in the default column the finger contact uses the pads’ own coefficient of 2.0. The 2.1× column (μ = 2.0 with priority) is a control for that switch: SmolVLA 88% at default against 84% in the control.
  5. Videos are SmolVLA trials of the benchmark, run again from the same initial state and seed and compared with the benchmark’s record. Most are identical to it step for step; where the simulator’s rendering is not bit-reproducible, the video is a re-recording with the original outcome and says so. Videos are drawn from the saved simulator states at 2× real time. Viewpoint and resolution are for the reader; the policy sees LIBERO’s own camera images. The blue target marks in the plate and bowl videos are drawn into the video only.
  6. π0.5 is not reproducible trial for trial across processes: two independent runs of the microwave task under default physics agree on success or failure in 48 of 50 initial states.
  7. The single-parameter chart takes the run with the most trials, then the longer one. The replanning chart takes two runs with the same step limit. For π0.5 on the drawer at 40 N these are different runs of one condition: 14/50 and 12/50 within 300 steps.
  8. Failure classes for the drawer come from contact logs. Blocked: the gripper touches the handle (more than 1 N) in at least 30% of the last 100 steps and the drawer moves less than 2 mm in them. Slipped: contact in fewer than 30% of those steps, no progress. Slow: still moving at the end. The log cannot tell a finger slipping off the handle from the policy letting go.
  9. Door dry friction: median hinge torque applied by SmolVLA while in contact rises from 1.0 N·m at default to 9.5 N·m at 12 N·m of friction (π0.5: 1.3 to 8.3 N·m). At 8 N·m the door has covered a median 83 to 97% of its travel in failed trials. For scale, a scripted full-scale push from the policy’s own contact point holds 20 to 24 N·m against a locked door. The scripted push only tests whether the door closes, not whether the mug is inside.
